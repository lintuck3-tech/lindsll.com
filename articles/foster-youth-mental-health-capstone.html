Interventions to Improve Mental Health Amongst Foster Youth.

A comprehensive literature review of five trauma-informed and evidence-based interventions for foster youth experiencing PTSD, including the service gaps that shape access to care.

Lindsey Tuck & Kimberlee Duncan-Rodriguez · San Francisco State University · 2021

01

The question

Foster youth experience trauma and PTSD at disproportionately high rates, yet choosing an intervention is only part of the challenge. This project asked three connected questions: which evidence-informed interventions improve mental health and reduce PTSD symptoms, which factors contribute to their success, and which service limitations prevent foster youth from benefiting.

02

The approach

We critically and systematically reviewed 45 peer-reviewed articles published between 2000 and 2021. The five interventions were selected using evidence ratings and child-welfare relevance from the California Evidence-Based Clearinghouse. We compared populations, methods, outcomes, treatment timelines, and limitations; no new human subjects were involved.

The right intervention is only as effective as the consistency of care surrounding it.

03

What we found

All five interventions showed value, but their strongest uses and limitations differed. TF-CBT and CPP received high child-welfare relevance scores; PCIT, PE, and EMDR received medium scores. Fit depended on age, symptoms, caregiver involvement, treatment length, and whether trained providers and consistent care were available.

01

PCIT

Targets noncompliance, aggression, disruptive behavior, and caregiver-child attachment.

02

TF-CBT

Reduces traumatic stress and addresses shame or distorted beliefs with caregiver support.

03

CPP

Supports children ages 0–5 through caregiver-child attachment, safe behavior, and affect regulation.

04

PE

Uses structured exposure with adolescents to reduce PTSD, depression, anger, guilt, and anxiety.

05

EMDR

Uses an eight-phase process to reprocess traumatic memories and reduce PTSD, anxiety, and fear.

04

Why access matters

Barriers appeared at every level. At the micro level, referral and service use varied by age, race and ethnicity, trauma history, stigma, trust, and prior experiences with therapy. At the mezzo level, placement changes disrupted providers and continuity while screening, local availability, and child-welfare-specific training remained uneven. At the macro level, fragmented systems, school-district responsibility, Medicaid reimbursement, eligibility rules, and visit limits restricted care.

MezzoPlacement disruption, screening, provider continuity
MacroSystem fragmentation, Medicaid, eligibility limits

05

What this project demonstrates

This paper shows why foster youth need access to evidence-based mental- health services. Each intervention reviewed had its own specialty and treatment approach, matching the different symptoms, ages, developmental needs, and caregiver contexts foster youth commonly experience. Just as important, the review shows that evidence-based care only works when young people can access it consistently. Continuity across therapists, placements, and care settings can determine whether treatment is sustained, trusted, and effective. Improving that consistency and continuity of care is one of the paper’s clearest recommendations.

A note on scope: The review assessed five selected interventions and a defined set of trauma-related symptoms. Treatment overlap and co-occurring needs make it difficult to attribute every improvement to one mechanism, and findings specific to child-welfare populations may not generalize to other youth.
