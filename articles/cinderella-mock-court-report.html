01

What a court report is

In a child-welfare case, a court report is written to inform the judge and assigned attorneys about the agency’s investigation, the safety concerns it identified, and its recommendations for why the agency believes a case needs to be opened. A recommendation to open a case means the agency determined that the child’s safety would remain at risk without further intervention. During the hearing, the investigator is present to answer questions the judge or attorneys may have about the investigation and the information in the report.

A strong report documents observed facts, reported statements, and professional assessment. It should be detailed enough to support a decision while remaining objective, organized, readable, and clear about where each piece of information came from.

The report shows what information was collected and assessed, how it was used to determine safety threats, and how the agency developed recommendations to help keep children safe.

02

What information is required

The exact requirements vary by court and jurisdiction, but a detention report generally combines identifying information, legal history, investigative findings, safety analysis, and recommendations. For this mock report, I included the same major categories found in actual court reports.

01

People and legal status

Child whereabouts, parents or guardians, attorneys, ICWA inquiry, legal relationships, and prior history.

02

Current safety concerns

The reason for the hearing, alleged harm, immediate danger, and the need for continued detention.

03

Evidence and sources

Interviews, witness statements, observations, records, photographs, and other supporting attachments.

04

Planning and recommendations

Services offered, relative-placement efforts, visitation, assessment, and requested court orders.

03

How the information is gathered

A child-welfare investigation generally begins with a referral from a mandated reporter. As the investigation develops, additional risk factors and safety threats may be discovered. Information is gathered through direct interviews with the children and caregivers, separate interviews with household members, collateral contacts with other people who may have relevant knowledge, home visits, records requests, and observation of the living environment.

Information from multiple witnesses, involved individuals, and outside agencies contributes to the evidence used for assessment. Common sources include schools, mental health providers, law enforcement, AOD treatment facilities, and extended family or friends. Statements that agree or conflict must be identified. The social worker must distinguish direct observations from reported information, assess the reliability and limits of the available evidence, and document efforts to locate relatives or consider less restrictive safety plans.

InterviewChild, caregiver, household members, and witnesses
ObserveHome conditions, behavior, safety hazards, and interactions
VerifyRecords, dates, physical evidence, and collateral accounts
AssessRisk, protective capacity, services, placement, and next steps

04

Building the mock report

I used Disney's 1950 version of Cinderella as a sampled storyline, adding fictional embellishments and details that would have been assessed during an investigation. Every event is placed within the same 1950 timeline, when the movie was originally released. The fictional investigation begins on August 7, the morning after the royal ball, when the Grand Duke arrives with the glass slipper and learns that Cinderella has been locked in her room. Cinderella is interviewed and removed that day, and the detention hearing takes place three days later on August 10. This keeps the mock timeline consistent with the 72-hour detention-hearing requirement following a child’s removal and illustrates the fast pace and pressure the investigator works under.

The report develops the story through interviews with Cinderella, Lady Tremaine, Anastasia, Drizella, the Grand Duke, the Fairy Godmother, and other collateral contacts. It also uses observations and attachments, including the exterior door lock, the torn dress and necklace, a detailed chore schedule, death certificates, and a mock guardianship order. Each detail is tied back to the assessment rather than appearing as an isolated fact.

A note on scope: This is a fictional educational exercise based on animated characters. It is not a real court filing, legal opinion, or record of an actual child-welfare case.

05

What this project demonstrates

This project illustrates the breadth of a child-welfare investigation. The work can move from an initial referral and first contact through investigative interviewing, collecting and verifying evidence, and ongoing risk and safety assessments. When safety threats require removal, the investigator must identify a shelter or placement while continuing the investigation. The investigation is then summarized in a report with recommendations for the court, for review by the judge and assigned attorneys.

The role of an investigator is important and intense, with real-world impact and consequences. It calls for good judgment, careful adherence to law and policy, collaboration with stakeholders, and the ability to remain calm under pressure. These demands show how multifaceted the work is and how much skill it requires.

Removing a child from their home is never taken lightly. Child-welfare investigators must balance the need to protect children from unsafe home environments with the impact that removal itself can have, recognizing that there is no ideal outcome in these situations.
